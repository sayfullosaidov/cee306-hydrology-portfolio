Overview¶

Overland flow simulates water flowing over a hillslope and is one aspect that makes up the runoff portion of the water balance,

$$ \Delta S = P-ET-R $$

where $\Delta S$ is change in storage (like in groundwater and lakes), $P$ is precipitation, $ET$ is evapotranspiration, and $R$ is runoff.

It is important to remember that there are other dynamics like ponding, infiltration, and subsurface flow, but overland flow is one central piece in understanding runoff. In this model, runoff is any non-ET flux of water out of the control volume, which could be overland flow, subsurface flow, and/or infiltration.

  1. Overland flow, the kinematic wave equation and its derivation

The Kinematic Wave Equation (KWE)¶

We use the fact that the upper boundary of water is a free surface whose material derivative is zero, meaning that following a surface parcel, some function $F$ describing its state does not change with time. This gives us the following boundary condition: $$ \frac{DF}{Dt}=0 $$

The $D/Dt$ operator is known as the material derivative (the rate of change following a fluid particle), and is written as $$ \frac{D}{Dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\bar{u} \frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\bar{v} \frac{\partial}{\partial y}+\bar{w}\frac{\partial}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla $$ which means we are considering a parcel as it flows along a fluid pathline. If we consider the 2D picture above, we can evaluate this function at the free surface, $z=z_s(x,t)$, and at the bottom boundary, $z=z_b(x,t)$ (note that both of these surfaces are a function of space, $x$, and time, $t$). At $z=z_s$, we have $$ \frac{DF}{Dt}=\bar{u}\frac{\partial z_s}{\partial x}-\bar{w}+\frac{\partial z_s}{\partial t} = 0 $$ and for $z=z_b$, we would have the same, $$ \frac{DF}{Dt}=\bar{u}\frac{\partial z_b}{\partial x}-\bar{w}+\frac{\partial z_b}{\partial t} = 0 $$ however, at the bottom we assume the flow has no vertical velocity, meaning that $\bar{w} = 0$ (for the bottom surface only). With this substitution in mind, the two previous equations can be subtracted to obtain an equation in terms of water depth $h = z_s - z_b$: $$ \frac{DF}{Dt} = \bar{u}\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}-\bar{w}+\frac{\partial h}{\partial t} = 0 $$

Skipping some derivation, we can write an equation in the above form that describes overland flow: a continuity equation for the change in height of water at one point in time, $\partial h / \partial t$.

The analog for the first contributing term is horizontal change in flux, which describes how much more or less water is running downhill to our point. This flux of water, in dimensions of $[\text{L}^2 \text{T}^{-1}]$, is velocity times height, $V h$ - so the horizontal change in this would be $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(Vh)$.

The analog for the middle term, $\bar{w}$, is some simple source or sink of water (in dimensions $[\text{L} \text{T}^{-1}]$), which we denote as $q$.

There is no analog for the last term, because this is what we are trying to track, how the water depth changes over time. Thus, the final expression is $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(Vh)-q+\frac{\partial h}{\partial t}=0 $$

But how do we know the velocity of a river without any discharge measurements? Even with discharge measurements, we would need to know the cross-section of the river at that point in time to calculate the velocity, which changes as river stage (height of the water level) changes. In this scenario, the Manning equation is commonly used to estimate velocity, written as $$ V=\frac{\sqrt{S_0} h^{2/3}}{n} $$ where $S_0$ is the water-surface slope (dimensions $[\text{L} \text{ L}^{-1}]$), $h$ is the height of the water as defined above, and $n$ is Manning's coefficient (dimensions $[\text{T} \text{ L}^{-1/3}]$). The $n$ value is derived empirically, with surface roughness playing a large factor in $n$ for a river. Guide for Selecting Manning's Roughness Coefficients for Natural Channels and Flood Plains, USGS Water-Supply Paper 2339 (1989)

2. Numerical Methods in Hydrology¶

Especially in the computing age, equations like the KWE are well suited to be solved by numerical (rather than analytical) methods. If we can establish initial conditions and boundary conditions, then we can calculate the partial derivatives explicitly as finite differences. For $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$, that looks like adjacent $x -$ current $x$, and for $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}$, that looks like adjacent $t -$ current $t$.

We can conceptually set up a grid with $x$ (space) and $t$ (time) as seen in the figure below. This grid represents our hillslope with values changing in space and time, and it is well represented by arrays.

A couple performance checkpoints for numerical solutions are convergence and stability -- the numerical solution must approach the analytical solution, and errors must not successively grow in size with each calculation. Generally, finer grid scales (smaller $dt$ especially) make for numerical solutions that are closer to the analytical solution but require more computations.

Figure 2: A numerical grid representation of our hillslope in time (for any x-t equation).

Numerical KWE Solution¶

Now, we want to discretize the kinematic wave equation. If we substitute $V$ in the continuity equation with Manning's equation, we make $h$ our primary dependent variable $$ \frac{\partial h}{\partial t} + \frac{\sqrt{S_0}}{n}\frac{\partial (h^{5/3})}{\partial x} = 0 $$

Note that we are currently ignore $q$, but it is assumed to not have defined derivatives in time or space, so it can easily be added in later.

When we discretize an equation, we essentially treat all partial derivatives as finite differences (as explained above). For example, a Taylor series discretization for $h$ in time would look like $$ \frac{\partial h}{\partial t} = \frac{h_i^{j+1}-h_i^j}{\Delta t} $$ and in space would look like $$ \frac{\partial h}{\partial x} = \frac{h_{i+1}^{j}-h_i^j}{\Delta x} $$ The variable $i$ represents an index in space, while the $j$ represents an index in time (not an exponent). The final discretization of our continuity equation is $$ \frac{h_i^{j+1} - h_i^j}{\Delta t} + \frac{\sqrt{S_0}}{n}\frac{h_{i+1}^{j (5/3)} - h_i^{j (5/3)}}{\Delta x} = 0 $$ Solving for the $h$ at a future time, $h_i^{j+1}$, and including a source/sink term ($w$) gives the equation for our numerical solution: $$ h_i^{j+1} = h_i^j + w - \frac{\Delta t \sqrt{S_0}}{n}\frac{h_{i+1}^{j (5/3)} - h_i^{j (5/3)}}{\Delta x} $$ which is what will be used in the code. The plan is to write a function that takes in different setup parameters and solves $h$ along the hillslope (in $x$ and in time) - this is our numerical solution to the kinematic wave equation. The function also calculates the runoff (in dimensions $[\text{L} \text{T}^{-1}]$) at the bottom of the hill, which is simply the final position's velocity (see equation above for $V$) times $h$ (ignoring the $y$-direction) and we add a source/sink term ($w$) to account for rain input.

def run_num(L,s0,n,rd,rr,dt,dx):
    # run_num is short for runoff numerical solution
    # the inputs are hillsope length (L), slope (s0), Manning's n (n), rain duration (rd), rain rate (rr), 
    # time step (dt), and space step (dx) which is the distance between two simulated spots on the hill
    
    # define simulation time (90 minutes in seconds)
    st = 90*60
    
    # make 'lengths' for space (i) and time (j)
    time = 1+int(st/dt) 
    space = 1+int(L/dx)
    
    # initialize the time array
    t = [0]
    
    # create a 2D space-time array
    h = np.zeros([space,time])
    
    # create 1D array for runoff in L^2/t, missing width of stream
    runoff = np.zeros(time)
    
    # loop through the discretized equation
    for j in range(0,(time-1)):
        
        # append time (in seconds)
        t.append((j+1)*dt)
        
        # for this timestep, update every new value in space
        for i in range(0,(space-1)):
            
            # if no rain, rain input is 0
            if j*dt > rd:
                w = 0
                
            # if still raining, rain rate times the timestep
            else:
                w = rr*dt
            
            # calculate the adjacent head value in time and space using equation
            h[i+1,j+1] = h[i+1,j] + w - ((dt*math.sqrt(s0))*(h[i+1,j]**(5/3) - h[i,j]**(5/3))/(dx*n))
        
        # calculate the runoff at the farthest head location
        runoff[j] = ((math.sqrt(s0)*(h[(space-1),j+1])**(5/3))/n)
        
    return runoff, h, t

3. Energy in Open-Channel Flow¶

Consider the classic Bernoulli energy equation for two points along a flow: $$ z_1 + \frac{p_1}{\gamma} + \frac{\alpha_1 V_1^2}{2g} = z_2 + \frac{p_2}{\gamma} + \frac{\alpha_2 V_2^2}{2g} + h_L $$ where the sum of pressure, elevation, and velocity head is conserved. Looking at the following figure for overland (open-channel) flow,

Figure 3: Conceptual model for overland flow in the context of energy.

the conservation of energy is denoted by the energy gradient line (EGL) in the figure. The head loss, $h_L$ accounts for the change in slope (more on that below).

We note that the sum of pressure and elevation head at the lower elevation is equal to the height of the water, which we denote $h$ (currently this is $y$ in the figure), thus $$ z_2 + \frac{p_2}{\gamma} = h_2 $$ The same is almost true at the higher elevation, where the sum of pressure and elevation head is equal to the height of the water plus the increase in elevation $S_0 \Delta x$, thus $$ z_1 + \frac{p_1}{\gamma} = h_1 + S_0 \Delta x $$ Making these substitutions in our energy equation above, we obtain $$ h_1 + S_0 \Delta x + \frac{\alpha_1 V_1^2}{2g} = h_2 + \frac{\alpha_2 V_2^2}{2g} + h_L $$ The head loss is proportional to the change in slope of the EGL. Thus, we can write the friction slope $$ S_0 = \frac{h_L}{\Delta x} $$ which is used in Mannings' equation. Recall because of the KWE assumption, $S_0 = S_f$.

Q4 (20 pts, code, plots, text)

The analytical solution to the KWE can be broken up to time of reaching steady state, time while raining and at steady state, and time after the rain has stopped. If $r_r$ is the rainfall rate $[\text{L} \text{T}^{-1}]$, the two points breaking these three sections can be defined as $$ t_c = \left(\frac{L}{\frac{\sqrt{S_0}}{n}r_r^\frac{2}{3}}\right)^{\frac{3}{5}} $$ and $$ t_r = \text{rainfall duration} $$

Since there are two transitions, there are thus three analytical solutions for runoff.

The runoff $q$ $[\text{L}^2 \text{T}^{-1}]$ when $0 \le t \le t_c$ is: $$ q = \frac{\sqrt{S_0}}{n}\left(r_r t\right)^\frac{5}{3} $$ while for $t_c < t \le t_r$ it is: $$ q = \frac{\sqrt{S_0}}{n}\left(r_r t_c\right)^\frac{5}{3} = r_r L $$ and for $t>t_r$ it is: $$ q = r_r L - r_r\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{S_0}}{n}\right)^\frac{3}{5}q^{\frac{2}{5}}(t-t_r) $$

For that final recession portion, notice $q$ appears on both sides of the equation. This solution needs to be solved with root finding. A method brought up in lecture was Newton's Method, and you can use scipy.optimize.newton, see more information here. Remember that for this method you need to define a function f(q) and find the derivative of the function that you are trying to optimize. You also need an initial guess of the root.
